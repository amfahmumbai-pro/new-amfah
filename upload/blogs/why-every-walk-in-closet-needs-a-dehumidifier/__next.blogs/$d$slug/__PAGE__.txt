      <p class="mb-4">Most people design a walk-in closet around lighting, shelving, and layout, how it looks, not what's happening inside the air. But the one thing that quietly undoes all that planning is humidity, and it rarely gets a second thought until something's already ruined.</p>

      <h3 class="text-xl font-bold font-display text-brand-navy mt-8 mb-4">Why Closets Trap Moisture More Than Other Rooms</h3>
      <p class="mb-4">A walk-in closet is essentially a sealed box. It stays shut most of the day, gets almost no cross-ventilation, and unlike a bedroom or living room, nobody's opening a window in there. So if the rest of your home runs even slightly humid, which is common through the monsoon or in any coastal city, that moisture has nowhere to escape once it drifts in. It just sits, and builds.</p>
      <p class="mb-4">The EPA's position on this is simple: mold needs moisture to grow, full stop. A closet that stays closed and slightly damp for weeks at a time is close to ideal conditions for it.</p>

      <h3 class="text-xl font-bold font-display text-brand-navy mt-8 mb-4">It's Not Just Clothes</h3>
      <p class="mb-4">That's the part people underestimate. A walk-in closet usually holds more than clothing, leather bags, formal shoes, silk sarees, wool jackets, jewelry boxes, sometimes the wooden shelving itself. All of it is exposed to the same air, and natural materials like silk, wool, suede, and leather absorb moisture far more readily than synthetic fabrics do.</p>

      <h3 class="text-xl font-bold font-display text-brand-navy mt-8 mb-4">The Damage Sneaks Up on You</h3>
      <p class="mb-4">Humidity rarely wrecks anything overnight. It's slow, which is exactly why people miss it. First it's a faint musty smell when you open the door. Then a bag develops a light white bloom of fungus, or a pair of shoes loses its shine for no obvious reason. Wooden shelves start feeling faintly tacky to the touch. None of these look urgent in isolation, but by the time you notice two or three of them together, the moisture's usually been sitting there for weeks already.</p>
      <p class="mb-4">Left long enough, this shows up as:</p>
      <ul class="list-disc pl-6 my-6 space-y-2.5">
        <li class="leading-relaxed text-brand-gray-dark/90">Warped or swollen wooden panels and shelving</li>
        <li class="leading-relaxed text-brand-gray-dark/90">Peeling cabinet finishes</li>
        <li class="leading-relaxed text-brand-gray-dark/90">Mold in corners or along seams</li>
        <li class="leading-relaxed text-brand-gray-dark/90">A closet that never quite loses its damp smell, even after airing it out</li>
      </ul>

      <h3 class="text-xl font-bold font-display text-brand-navy mt-8 mb-4">Are Silica Packs and Moisture Absorbers Enough?</h3>
      <p class="mb-4">For a small space with mild humidity, sure, they help for a while. The problem is they saturate fast, especially through a long monsoon, and once they're full, they stop doing anything until replaced. If the room around the closet is genuinely humid, absorbers are treating a symptom, not the source.</p>

      <h3 class="text-xl font-bold font-display text-brand-navy mt-8 mb-4">What Actually Fixes This</h3>
      <p class="mb-4">A dehumidifier doesn't work shelf by shelf it brings down humidity across the whole room, which means the closet stops re-absorbing moisture from the air around it. Practically, that means clothes hold their fresh smell longer, leather stays supple instead of developing fungus, wood doesn't warp, and you stop getting that faint musty hit every time you open the door.</p>
      <p class="mb-4">The EPA recommends keeping indoor humidity between 30–50%, and below 60% at the outside limit. That's a reasonable target in most rooms, much harder to hold inside a sealed closet without something actively pulling moisture out.</p>

      <h3 class="text-xl font-bold font-display text-brand-navy mt-8 mb-4">A Few Habits Worth Building In Anyway</h3>
      <p class="mb-4">Even with a dehumidifier running, some basic habits go a long way:</p>
      <ul class="list-disc pl-6 my-6 space-y-2.5">
        <li class="leading-relaxed text-brand-gray-dark/90">Only hang clothes once they're completely dry even slightly damp fabric raises humidity inside a closed space</li>
        <li class="leading-relaxed text-brand-gray-dark/90">Leave a little breathing room between hangers instead of packing it tight</li>
        <li class="leading-relaxed text-brand-gray-dark/90">Crack the doors open for a few minutes every couple of days</li>
        <li class="leading-relaxed text-brand-gray-dark/90">Wipe down shelves periodically dust plus moisture is a common starting point for mold</li>
      </ul>

      <h3 class="text-xl font-bold font-display text-brand-navy mt-8 mb-4">Who Should Take This Seriously</h3>
      <p class="mb-4">If you're storing bridal wear, silk sarees, a real handbag collection, leather shoes, or seasonal clothing that sits untouched for months, this matters more than it does for an everyday wardrobe. Same if you're in Mumbai, Goa, or anywhere else that sees a proper monsoon, the humidity load is simply higher, and a bigger walk-in closet means more surface area exposed to it.</p>

      <h3 class="text-xl font-bold font-display text-brand-navy mt-8 mb-4">The Bottom Line</h3>
      <p class="mb-4">The closet is an investment, and so is everything hanging in it.
